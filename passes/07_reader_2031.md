# Pass 07 — The Reader in 2031

Persona: a safety-assurance engineer or researcher opening this 2026 supplementary
repository in 2031. By then, plausibly: ISO/PAS 8800 has been converted to a full
ISO 8800 (a PAS is a transitional publication type, valid ~6 years, so a conversion
or withdrawal by 2030-2031 is close to certain); ISO/SAE 21434 has a revision
underway or published; ISO/TS 5083 has matured toward an IS; the UNECE 2026 driverless
framework (ECE/TRANS/WP.29/2026/139) has produced actual in-service occurrence reports;
and 3D detector architectures have moved past SECOND / PointPillars. This reader asks
one question: **what in this work is durable, and what was of its moment?** — and, more
practically, **can I re-run the analysis against the standards as they now read, and can
I check the predictions the papers made?**

This pass answers three things: (a) what looks durable and why, (b) what is version-bound
and why, (c) what artefacts are missing that would have made the work re-checkable across
a standards revision. It reads both papers in full, the README, and REPO_AUDIT.md.

---

## The one-line verdict for 2031

The *ideas* survive a renumbering; the *repository* does not. Every load-bearing structural
claim in both papers is stated in a form that outlives clause numbers — but the machine-checkable
apparatus (`src/`, the 192 tests, the hard-coded clause strings) is a frozen 2024-2026 snapshot
with no version field, no external standards data that is actually loaded, and no crosswalk. So a
2031 reader can *re-read* the argument and find it still stands, but **cannot re-execute it against
the standards they actually hold in their hands.** The papers are re-checkable by a human against
the new editions; the code is not re-runnable against them.

---

## (a) What looks DURABLE — and why

These claims are phrased structurally (about relationships, measurement scales, and argument
topology) rather than about specific clause text. A 2031 reader re-doing the extraction against
ISO 8800:20xx and ISO/SAE 21434:20xx would still find them, because the underlying condition is
architectural, not editorial.

1. **The evidence-asymmetry finding at the V&V node (WAISE DP-2 / central finding).**
   The claim is that the evidence types required at the V&V goal are *disjoint by design* and
   carry *different measurement scales* — MC/DC coverage, scenario coverage, an uncertainty/AUROC
   value, and an attack-success rate — and that no standard prescribes a rule for combining them
   into one sufficiency judgement. This is a property of *having four concern-specific standards
   at all*, not of any clause. Renumbering ISO 8800's Annex B does not remove the fourth scale.
   Durable. This is the paper's strongest contribution and the one most likely to still be true
   (and still open) in 2031.

2. **The integration-induced vs open-methodological distinction (WAISE, Step 5, F-3/F-4 vs F-1/F-2/F-5).**
   The *category* — findings visible only between standard scopes vs framework-prescribed-but-value-deferred
   problems — is a reusable analytical lens. The WAISE discussion itself generalises it beyond automotive
   ("any domain that assures one component against several concern-specific standards, such as aviation or
   medical devices"). The lens survives any revision because it is about *how* to read a set of standards,
   not about their content.

3. **The concern-assignment gap (SAFECOMP position paper).**
   An unlabeled runtime anomaly carries no concern label (missing "step 1"), and no clause resolves
   per-concern re-evaluations into one judgment on the assurance argument (missing "step 2"). This is
   a topological claim about where concern-specific activities meet. A revision could *close* it (the
   paper predicts revised 8800/21434 could add exactly the assignment + resolution steps), but the
   *existence of the gap as of 2026* is a stable historical fact, and the *shape* of what would close
   it (requirements R1-R4: heterogeneous evidence, assignment, traceability/currency) is version-independent.

4. **The relationship-topology observations.** That the only normative cybersecurity↔safety bridge is
   the TARA impact-rating derivation (RQ-15-06 → ISO 26262-3), and that ISO/PAS 8800 Table 1-1 lists
   relationships with several standards but omits ISO/SAE 21434 — the *pattern* (cybersecurity is bridged
   only at the impact-rating level, not at the GSN/argument level) is a durable diagnosis even if the
   specific requirement ID or table number changes.

5. **The constructive ("build first, then examine") method.** The five-step methodology is a process,
   independent of which editions it is applied to. A 2031 reader could apply the same five steps to the
   new editions and it would still function.

Why these are durable: none of them depend on a clause *number*. They depend on the continued existence
of multiple concern-specific standards with disjoint evidence types and no combination rule — a condition
a revision is far more likely to *inherit* than to remove.

## (b) What looks VERSION-BOUND — and why

Everything below is pinned to a specific 2024-2026 document edition. When clause numbers shift, these
statements become claims about historical editions that the reader must manually re-verify — and the
repository gives no help in doing so.

1. **Every clause citation in both papers.** The WAISE paper is dense with edition-specific anchors:
   Annex B = G1-G6; cybersecurity case = ISO/SAE 21434 Cl.3.1.11; RQ-15-06; encompassing safety case
   = ISO 26262-2 Cl.6.4.8; SOTIF acceptance = ISO 21448 Cl.6.5; data quality = ISO 8800 Cl.8.4; TR 5469
   Cl.9.3.2/9.3.3; Tables A.8/A.9/A.10/A.13/A.14; Cl.14.8.3; Cl.15.8. The position paper adds ISO 21448
   Cl.13.4 as the field-result→argument mapping. **All of these will move on revision.** ISO/PAS 8800 in
   particular, on conversion to a full IS, is the single most likely document to renumber its clauses and
   restructure or expand Annex B — and Annex B is the paper's entire base structure.

2. **The "ISO/PAS 8800 omits ISO/SAE 21434 from Table 1-1" observation.** This is a PAS-specific artefact.
   Converting 8800 to a full standard is precisely the moment a committee would revisit its relationship
   table — and given papers like this one flagging the omission, adding ISO/SAE 21434 is a plausible
   revision. If it is added, this specific diagnosis (Discussion, "Implications for Assurance Practice")
   becomes obsolete. The *higher-level* point (no GSN-level bridge) may survive; the *table-level* point
   will likely not.

3. **"ISO/SAE 21434 has no clause that re-evaluates the cybersecurity case" (position paper).**
   The paper itself predicts a revision could add a re-evaluation case. This is the claim most exposed to
   being closed by the 21434 revision the reader in 2031 is likely holding.

4. **The exact counts: 9 goals, 7 decision points, 5 findings, 2 integration-induced.**
   These are locked to the current editions *and* to a single-assessor extraction (the paper is candid:
   Step 1 was one assessor, no inter-rater reliability). A revision that legitimately merges or splits a
   clause, or that adds an AI-specific re-assurance workflow (closing F-2) or AI quantitative targets
   (closing F-1), changes the counts. The numbers are an artefact of the 2024-2026 corpus, not invariants.

5. **The UNECE 2026 framing.** The position paper's entire motivation rests on the June 2026 WP.29
   framework being *first-ever* and freshly adopted, obliging field reporting with "no defined procedure
   to turn a field anomaly into a current judgment." By 2031 that framework is operational, probably
   amended, and — critically — has produced real reports. The motivation is time-stamped; the gap it
   points at may or may not still be open.

6. **The case-study specifics.** SECOND (paper text) / PointPillars (empirical substitution), the deep
   ensemble, CARLA, SOTIF-PCOD (547 frames, 22 weather configs), AUROC 0.982, 22.0% retention with no
   false acceptances. These are architecture-, dataset-, and seed-bound, and the headline numbers are
   *synthetic outputs calibrated to published distributions*, not measurements. By 2031 the detector is
   dated and the numbers are illustrative history. The *argument structure* is architecture-agnostic and
   survives; the *instantiated evidence leg* does not.

Why these are version-bound: each is an assertion about the *content or numbering* of a specific edition.
The moment the edition changes, the assertion needs re-checking, and nothing in the repo automates that.

## (c) What the 2031 reader WANTS and CANNOT GET

This is the core of the pass. Below is what a reader trying to *re-validate the work against the current
standards* would reach for, and find absent.

### C1. A versioned, machine-readable standards registry (clauses as data, keyed by edition)
The reader wants to open a data file, change "ISO/PAS 8800:2024" to "ISO 8800:2029", update the clause
references, and re-run. **They cannot.** The clause references are hard-coded as Python string literals
inside `src/standards/*.py` (`Clause(reference="Cl.8.4", ...)`), with no `edition`/`version` field on the
`Clause` or `Standard` data model. There *is* a `configs/standards.yaml`, but REPO_AUDIT.md confirms it is
**inert — not loaded by any code or test**; it is an unsynchronised parallel copy. So the one file that
looks like "the standards as data you could edit" does nothing. Re-extraction against a revision means
hand-editing Python across five modules and hoping the 192 count-assertions still make sense.

### C2. A crosswalk / clause-migration table (2024-2026 → later editions)
The reader holds a different edition and wants to know: "the paper's DP-2 cites ISO 8800 Cl.8; where did
that go?" There is no mapping. Worse, REPO_AUDIT.md documents a *within-2026* label drift that already
breaks cross-referencing: the code uses `I-1..I-7` and `Gap-1..Gap-5`, the paper uses `DP-1..DP-7` and
`F-1..F-5`, the README uses a third mixture, and the GSN YAML still says `Gap-2`. Three vocabularies for
two object sets, no crosswalk, in 2026. A 2031 reader with fuzzy memory of which prefix meant what, and a
renumbered standard on top, is doubly lost.

### C3. Version-parameterised (structural) tests instead of frozen counts
The reader wants tests that assert the *durable* claims — "the pattern extends Annex B without removing a
retained goal", "G5 receives claims from all normative standards", "at least one finding is invisible from
any single standard" — so that a revision can be dropped in and the *structural* invariants re-checked.
Instead the suite asserts brittle magnitudes: `== 9`, `== 7`, `== 5`, `{"Gap-3","Gap-4"}`. Against a
revised corpus these fail *by construction* even when the structural claim still holds, and there is no way
to distinguish "the finding is gone" from "the clause was renumbered." The tests lock the analysis to the
snapshot rather than protecting the durable claims through it.

### C4. A falsifiable prediction registry with a checking protocol
Both papers make explicit generalisation predictions:
 - WAISE: the II/OM distinction "applies to any domain that assures one component against several
   concern-specific standards, such as aviation or medical devices."
 - Position paper: "We expect the same gap wherever one component is assured against several
   concern-specific standards."

The 2031 reader — who by now may have aviation (ARP4761/DO-178C-style) or medical (IEC 62304 / ISO 14971)
multi-concern cases to test against — wants these stated as falsifiable propositions with a defined check:
what would confirm the gap in a new domain, what would refute it. **Neither the papers nor the repo provide
a protocol, a template, or a single worked cross-domain instance.** The predictions are assertions of
expectation, not registered, checkable hypotheses. The reader cannot tell whether a 2031 aviation case that
*lacks* the gap refutes the paper or is simply out of scope.

### C5. An in-service-report ingestion path for the position paper's gap
The position paper's whole premise is that WP.29 field reports will arrive with no procedure to turn them
into a current judgment on the safety case. By 2031 those reports *exist*. The reader wants to take a real
occurrence report and run it through the paper's requirements (R1 heterogeneous evidence, R2 assignment,
R3 traceability/currency, R4) to see whether the gap materialised as predicted. **There is no artefact for
this at all** — the position paper is text-only by design (REPO_AUDIT §5 confirms no `src/` backing), and
`src/` has no anomaly-assignment or report-ingestion module. The most testable prediction in the whole body
of work has zero tooling to test it.

### C6. A "which findings survive which revision" statement
The repo has `base_pattern_sensitivity.py` ("which findings survive base-pattern changes") — tantalisingly
close, but it tests robustness to *changing the GSN base pattern*, not robustness to *a standards revision*.
The reader wants the analogue keyed to editions: if ISO 8800 adds AI quantitative targets, F-1 closes; if
21434 adds a re-evaluation case, the position-paper gap narrows; if 8800 Table 1-1 adds 21434, the Table 1-1
observation dies. A short, explicit "revision sensitivity" map would let a 2031 reader instantly see which
of the five findings a given amendment has already closed. It does not exist; the reader must reconstruct it
by hand from prose.

### C7. Reproducible empirical evidence against a current detector
The reader wants to re-run the G5/G6 evidence on a 2031-era detector to see if the ensemble-disagreement
result holds for modern architectures. **They cannot.** REPO_AUDIT §7.1 confirms `data/empirical_results/`
is measured output from an *external, unpublished* PointPillars run ("P5"), with no training code, no raw
KITTI/nuScenes data, and no command sequence in the repo. The empirical claims were not reproducible in 2026
and are certainly not re-runnable against a new detector in 2031. Dependencies are also unpinned (lower
bounds only, no lockfile), so even the deterministic synthetic path is not guaranteed byte-identical across a
five-year gap in numpy/torch. The `make gsn-install` target is Linux-only on a documented Windows host.

### C8. A provenance/edition stamp on every generated artefact and figure
The reader wants each figure and table to say "generated from ISO/PAS 8800:2024, ISO/SAE 21434:2021, ...".
The generated outputs carry only a `generated` timestamp; `docs/figures/*.png` and `data/carla_configs/`
have no provenance README at all (REPO_AUDIT §4). A 2031 reader cannot tell, from an artefact in isolation,
which editions it encodes.

---

## What this means for the 2031 reader, in one paragraph

The intellectual contribution ages well: the evidence-asymmetry-at-V&V finding, the
integration-induced-vs-methodological distinction, and the concern-assignment gap are stated at a level
that a renumbering cannot touch, and a 2031 reader re-doing the analysis by hand would very likely reach the
same structural conclusions. The *evidentiary apparatus* ages badly: it is a hand-coded 2024-2026 snapshot
with no version field, an inert config that only looks editable, count-based tests that break on any revision,
a three-way label drift with no crosswalk, and empirical numbers that were never reproducible. The two most
future-facing elements — the cross-domain generalisation prediction and the UNECE field-report concern-assignment
gap — are exactly the two with the least tooling: both are asserted in prose and neither can be mechanically
checked against the 2031 world they were written to anticipate. The single change that would most improve the
work's 2031 re-checkability is to promote the standards from hard-coded Python strings to a loaded, edition-keyed
data registry, and to re-express the tests as structural invariants over that registry rather than as frozen
counts — turning "self-consistency check of a 2026 snapshot" into "re-executable check against whatever edition
the reader holds."
