Where the number comes from

The WAISE paper gives one reason per standard: an AI-based perception component falls within the scope of all four at once. ISO 26262 applies because a perception failure can contribute to ASIL D hazardous events at SAE Level 4; ISO 21448 (SOTIF, the safety of the intended functionality) because the intended function can produce hazardous behaviour without a fault; ISO/SAE 21434 because the sensor data are vulnerable to adversarial manipulation; ISO/PAS 8800 because the component is a trained neural network. ISO/IEC TR 5469 is cited as an informative technical report and is not counted.

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

A scope choice of the papers. ISO/TS 5083 (safety at the level of the automated driving system), ISO 26262 Parts 9 to 12, UL 4600, the EU AI Act and UNECE R155/R156 are outside it. The position paper works with three of the four: ISO 21448, ISO/PAS 8800 and ISO/SAE 21434, each of which defines an operation-phase activity that uses runtime monitor evidence.

Goals in the integrated GSN argument

1 reformulated: G15 retained: G2, G3, G4, G5, G62 added: G7, G81 added, undeveloped: G9

Where the number comes from

Step 3 of the WAISE method starts from the GSN pattern in ISO/PAS 8800 Annex B: a top-level goal G1 and five sub-goals G2 to G6. The rule is to attach claims from the other standards to an existing goal where the assurance domain matches, and to create a new goal only where no equivalent exists. G1 is reformulated to claim the integrated safety and cybersecurity requirements, because the strategy now argues over four assurance domains. G7 (SOTIF residual risk) and G8 (cybersecurity risk) are added because each claims that a residual risk is acceptable, which is a judgement rather than a verification activity. G9 (re-assurance after a modification) is added and left undeveloped, because no single standard prescribes the complete re-assurance workflow.

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

The base pattern is a choice; the count follows from it and from the rule above. The WAISE paper names an alternative top-level structure, the concern hierarchy of Warg and Skoglund, and keeps the Annex B structure because it preserves the activity-based decomposition and keeps each branch traceable to one standard.

Evidence types at G5, the V&V goal

ISO/SAE 21434ISO 21448ISO 26262ISO/PAS 8800

Where the number comes from

G5 claims that the verification and validation (V&V) evidence is sufficient. It is the goal to which all four standards contribute, and the case-study evidence set has one evidence type from each: (a) MC/DC structural coverage of the surrounding non-AI software (ISO 26262), (b) SOTIF triggering-condition coverage in scenario testing (ISO 21448), (c) deep-ensemble disagreement as an uncertainty indicator, measured by AUROC (ISO/PAS 8800), and (d) adversarial penetration testing, measured as attack success rate (ISO/SAE 21434).

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

The count follows from the scope: one evidence type per contributing standard. The result is what the WAISE paper finds about them: the types are disjoint by design, each on its own measurement scale, and no standard prescribes how they combine into a single sufficiency claim at G5 (decision point DP-2).

7

Decision points

3 structural2 terminological2 methodological

Where the number comes from

Step 4 of the WAISE method (junction-point analysis) examines each goal where claims from two or more standards meet, and records a decision point wherever the standards defer to application context, so that the engineer has to decide. In this argument six goals are junctions: G1, G2, G4, G5, G6, G8. DP-7 sits at G3, to which only one of the four standards contributes: there ISO/PAS 8800 meets the informative ISO/IEC TR 5469 on data sufficiency. DP-3 and DP-5 also attach to G7, to which only one of the four standards contributes: DP-3 concerns the AI-error terminology used at both G2 and G7, and DP-5 the boundary between G7 and G8. G4 is a junction without a decision point. The paper distinguishes three types: terminological (different terms for the same concept), methodological (different methods for the same objective), and structural (frameworks requiring decisions that no standard prescribes). A project can settle the first two by agreeing on a vocabulary or a method.

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

A result of the analysis: the count is what the examination found. The split by type follows the paper's prose; the paper's Table 4 types DP-2 as both structural and methodological, which ERRATA.md records.

Computed from the code. Check: decision_points.py · ERRATA.md, entry 1

Findings

2 integration-induced: F-3, F-43 open methodological problems: F-1, F-2, F-5

Where the number comes from

Step 5 of the WAISE method sorts the findings into two categories. An integration-induced finding emerges only between the scopes of the standards and cannot be detected from any one of them alone: F-3, adversarial inputs that exploit a functional insufficiency fall between G7 and G8 and belong to neither; F-4, no standard requires evaluating the combined residual risk across domains for the release decision. An open methodological problem is one where the standards prescribe a framework, but no published method supplies what it needs: application-specific quantitative acceptance criteria (F-1) and data acceptance criteria (F-5) for AI components, and a complete re-assurance workflow after an over-the-air update (F-2).

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

A result of the analysis. As a further check, not reported in either paper, this repository derives F-3 and F-4 under two argument structures, Activity-based (this work) and Concern hierarchy (Warg & Skoglund): both findings arise under each.

Missing steps in operation (position paper)

Assignment of an anomaly to a concernResolution of the concern-specific results into one judgement

Where the number comes from

In operation, a runtime monitor can report an abnormal output, for example a scene region where detections drop. The anomaly carries no concern label: rain and a spoofing attack can produce the same missing detections. ISO 21448, ISO/PAS 8800 and ISO/SAE 21434 each define an operation-phase activity within their own concern, but no clause in these standards assigns the anomaly to a concern (step 1), and none resolves the concern-specific results into one judgement on the assurance argument (step 2).

Scope choice, result, or encoding?

Argued from clause text. The two steps are kept apart because labelling every anomaly would close the first and leave the second. The paper's position is that closing the gap is an in-service re-evaluation of the safety case, not an in-vehicle runtime function, and it states four requirements for a method that closes it: R1 heterogeneous evidence, R2 assignment, R3 traceability and currency, R4 modification. Each is necessary; the paper does not claim the set is sufficient.

From the position paper's text; no code computes it. Check: the anomaly notebook · the comparison page
